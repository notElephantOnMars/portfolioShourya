About

Background, in brief.

I'm a mechanical engineering student who gravitates toward the automotive and mechatronics side of the field — where drivetrain, sensors, and control logic all have to work together. I'm communicative and collaborative by nature, and I bring a steady, positive attitude to team environments, especially when a project hits the inevitable snag. What drives me is genuine curiosity: I want to understand a system well enough to improve it, and I care about the people I'm building it with as much as the build itself. I studied Mechanical Engineering at the University of Calgary, graduating in 2029. Since then I've worked across machine design, controls, and manufacturing.

I like projects that force a full loop: sketch, simulate, machine, assemble, debug the firmware at 1am, and get it working on the bench. Most of what's below started as a napkin drawing.

REV A

Door Hinge - University of Calgary Solar Car Team

A pop-out and up door opening design complimenting the aeroshell of the 6th gen solar car Schulich Helios.

GD&TANSYS FEASolidWorksCollaboration

Purpose

This was the first mechanical design project I took upon myselft to complete. The purpose of this type of door hinge relies behind the complicated shape of the side skirts on our car - see more details in the breakdown below.

Design

The hinge was loaded in the worst case scenario, where the expected force of 20 lbf for the door was applied at the furthest point from the hinge. The beauty of this design is its repeatability, and attention to deformation under the load of the door- see the full write-up for more details.

Building it in real life

A combination of off the shelf parts, waterjet, and recycling previous parts was used for this design process to make these hinges work- see the deep dive below to see exactly how.

What I learned

This was my first project in solar car, and there were many things I did not account for during this design process. A lack of design reviews prohibited an efficient design from being made, as collaboration increases creavity and innovation of every design. Some issues for this design include mounting arms from the door to the hinge, and a loose, almost wobbly hinge. I am working on fixing this currently and an increased importance in design reviews will surely help me achieve a better design.

REV B

Sensor-Feedback Delivery Robot

An autonomous delivery robot that navigates an obstacle course, picks up a 3D-printed payload, and drops it at a delivery zone — repeating as many loops as possible in a 10-minute window.

OnshapeEmbedded3D Printing

Purpose

The challenge was to build a robot that could run a course of obstacles, pick up a 3D-printed payload, and deliver it to a marked delivery area — then repeat that cycle as many times as possible inside a 10-minute window. Speed, reliability, and repeatability mattered as much as getting the pickup and drop-off right.

Design

Navigation relied on two HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors for wall following through the course, paired with an IR emitter/receiver setup for line following. I did the overall CAD model for the robot, including the full assembly of every component I could source online — chassis, sensor mounts, payload gripper, and drivetrain — so everything fit and moved together before it was ever built.

Building it in real life

Getting the motors to work together reliably took a lot of iteration — see the full write-up for the specific issues we ran into and how we solved each one.

What I learned

Balancing two different sensor feedback loops (wall-following distance control and line-following steering) taught me a lot about tuning control logic for competing priorities, and about how much fabrication tolerance matters when a robot has to repeat the same run over and over under a time limit.

REV C

Aston Martin One-77 — Full Surface Model

A from-scratch SolidWorks rebuild of the Aston Martin One-77, complete with a custom steering system that actually turns the wheels.

SolidWorksSurface ModellingTutorial-Guided

Purpose

I wanted to push my SolidWorks skills up a steep learning curve, so over two months I set out to rebuild the Aston Martin One-77 entirely from scratch. I followed a guided tutorial from LearnSOLIDWORKS.com, led by Jan-Willem Zuyderduyn, rather than working from my own design — a deliberate choice to learn advanced surfacing technique before applying it to my own projects.

Design

The body relies heavily on 3D projected curves to raise the level of surface detail, and ruled surfaces to fill patches and add debossed details like the headlight backplates and door handles. I pushed past the tutorial's base scope by designing a custom steering system so the front wheels actually turn.

Building it in real life

This was a full digital build: assembling the tires, rims, and steering system into the rest of the modelled car, and using surface modelling throughout to capture the intricate character lines seen on modern automotive bodies.

What I learned

I couldn't rebuild this car from scratch without the tutorial yet — but that's not really the point. It gave me the fundamentals of surface modelling, which is exactly the barrier that was stopping me from starting my own personal projects with well-designed, complex components.

REV D

Custom Drawer Tower

A 3D printed drawer tower, designed using SolidWorks, fitted with 15 small drawers, 1 medium, and 1 large drawer.

SolidWorksFor Fun3D Printing

Purpose

Starting on my arduino kit, I noticed how messy it would get to play with different sensors, switches, and resistors. This motivated me to build my own drawer tower, where I could control exactly how many drawers I needed, and how much desk space it would occupy.

Design

I noticed the long print time when I first created the design. This was due to an overly high infill density, and an inefficient structure for space optimization. I used a 5% infill density and removed material between supporting shelves to reduce 2 hours of print time! I also added pokeballs as door handles for fun!

Building it in real life

The build was not hard to assemble because of it's simplicity but more importantly, my attention to tolerance. The drawers had a profile that was 0.3 mm lower than the hold profiles in the hull of the drawer tower. This ensured a smooth fit for all my drawers as shown in the video.

What I learned

I use the 0.3 mm tolerance for all sliding parts in my prints now as they fit precisely. This project taught me to minimize print times by decreasing infill density, and removing uneccesary material to ensure cost and time efficiency. P.S. Mom liked it so much I have to print one for her now :).
